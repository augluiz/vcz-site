The ground truth

Brazil is not one market.
It is many, and they disagree.

Most entry plans we see are not wrong about the product. They are wrong about the structure — how goods actually reach a Brazilian shelf, what retail charges for the privilege, and how little of that appears in any price list. These are the six things that vary most from one operation to the next — and that most often break a plan built from abroad.

01

Trade allowances: the format varies

Brazilian retail expects commercial funds on top of your price, but the format shifts by chain and by category: listing fees to open a code, promotional allowances, co-op advertising, rebates tied to volume. It is rarely on a price list, and it comes out of your margin. A price that looks healthy in a European spreadsheet may not survive those terms.

02

Payment terms move your cash position

Payment on delivery is not the norm, and the terms actually granted vary considerably by channel and by negotiation — they are part of the commercial deal, not a favour. The practical consequence is that your working capital requirement tends to be materially larger than the order value suggests, and that gap is where underfunded entries stumble.

03

The same product costs different amounts per state

Brazil's main consumption tax is levied at state level, with its own rules for movements between states and special regimes for whole product categories. The result is that landed cost and viable shelf price vary by destination state. Pricing Brazil as a single territory produces a number that is wrong almost everywhere in it.

04

National coverage rarely comes from one partner

For most categories distribution is regional, and so are many of the retail chains. A partner who is strong in the South can be irrelevant in the Northeast. Signing one distributor to cover a country of continental scale tends to be expensive; coverage is normally built region by region.

05

Channels behave like separate markets

Large-format retail, neighbourhood stores, cash and carry, pharmacy, foodservice, e-commerce and marketplaces each have their own price expectation, pack logic and margin structure. Cash and carry in particular has no clean equivalent in most European markets, and a product designed for one channel frequently cannot be sold into another without being reworked.

06

There is no "Brazilian consumer"

Income, social class, pack size preference, brand loyalty, channel mix and price sensitivity differ sharply — between regions and within each of them. A launch calibrated for middle-class São Paulo tells you little about the interior of the Northeast, and little even about the high-income audience in that same city. Choosing where and for whom to enter first is a strategic decision, not a logistics one.

Where you are starting from

Three ways companies
arrive at this page.

The work is the same discipline from any of them — reading a market properly before committing money to it. The starting point is what changes.

Door one

Selling in Brazil

You manufacture or own a brand and you want your product on Brazilian shelves. The question is never only whether there is demand — it is which route to market your margin can actually survive, and which partner will not quietly park your product.

  • Whether the category has real demand, and in which regions it concentrates
  • Which route fits: importing distributor, direct to retail, cash and carry, e-commerce, marketplace or a mix
  • What your shelf price would have to be, and what that leaves you after trade allowances and terms
  • Who the credible partners are per region, and how to qualify them before signing
  • Which region to enter first, and what a pilot should prove before you scale

Door two

Sourcing from Brazil

You are a buyer, importer or distributor abroad looking for Brazilian supply. The difficulty is rarely finding companies that claim to export — it is telling apart the ones that can actually hold volume, quality and documentation from the ones that cannot.

  • Mapping suppliers by category and region
  • Qualification of the contact before you commit: track record, references, export readiness
  • Commercial intermediation and negotiation in Portuguese, where local representation is engaged
  • On-site follow-up where the project calls for it, agreed case by case

Door three

Already here, underperforming

You entered Brazil and the numbers never arrived. This is the most common of the three, and the diagnosis is usually not the one head office assumes. It is also the closest to our core consulting work — the same analysis, entered through a different door.

  • Whether the problem is price positioning, the partner, assortment or coverage
  • Whether trade investment is buying anything, or just funding someone's promotion
  • Whether the product was ever adapted to how Brazilian shoppers actually buy
  • Whether your distributor is selling you or storing you
  • A prioritised reading of where result is leaking, and what to fix first

How it works

Assessment first.
Commitment after.

You do not need to commit to a large project to find out whether Brazil is worth it. You start with the market assessment: scope, timeline and deliverable agreed before any work begins. At the end of it you have what you need to decide — and if the decision is not to proceed, it ends there, with nothing left open. The phases are also modular: you can engage just one of them — a standalone piece of market research, for instance — without buying the whole sequence.

01

Market assessmentEntry point

A structured read of the Brazilian market for your specific category: where demand sits and how it differs by region, who already competes there, what price levels the shelf carries today, and which channels matter for a product like yours. You finish this phase able to decide whether to proceed — with evidence, not impressions.

02

Channel and pricing structure

Price built backwards from the shelf rather than forwards from your factory: what the product has to retail at to be credible, what the channel takes, what trade allowances and payment terms will cost you, and what remains. Alongside it, the route-to-market options with the trade-off of each laid out — control versus reach, margin versus speed.

03

Partner mapping and qualification

Identification of distributors, representatives and retail partners by region and channel, then qualification before you commit — what they actually cover, which competing lines they carry, whether they have the structure to execute. You receive the qualified shortlist with a recommendation; conducting the approach itself is part of local representation, in the next phase.

04

Local representationOptional

If you want someone in Brazil carrying the plan forward rather than handing it to you, this continues: approaching the selected partners and conducting the conversations in Portuguese, accompanying negotiations, following the pilot, monitoring performance against what was agreed and correcting course. It is a separate decision taken after the assessment — never a condition of it, and never the reason the assessment reaches a particular conclusion.
